We want more kids walking out with a book they’ll finish

When we sat with librarians, they said it plainly: if they recommend a book, kids usually read it. The hard part is remembering, in a crowded few minutes at the desk, what that child liked last time — and what’s still on the shelf today.

This is a simple tool so librarians can help more kids — without asking children to log into an app, and without asking librarians to work harder.

What the librarians told us

Kids trust a librarian’s recommendation. Librarians mostly work from memory and stories about other kids: “If they liked this, they often like that.”

They offered us two things they already have: the history of what students borrowed, and the electronic card catalog. That’s enough to start helping.

We’re not trying to replace librarians. We’re trying to give them a better notebook for the rush before school, the last minutes of specials, and Friday afternoon checkout.

Our goal is to give librarians the tools to scale their impact without scaling — while reducing their workload.

What we’re proposing

A screen at the librarian’s desk — not in a child’s hands.

  • Look up a student.
  • See a short list of next books that fit what they’ve borrowed before.
  • Only show titles we actually have copies of.
  • Let the librarian choose what to say, in their own voice.

If it helps, it can draft a couple of talking points. If those aren’t there, the book list still works. The child still hears a person.

How it works

Borrow history and the card catalog go in. A short list comes out for the librarian. The student never sees the system.

A normal day with it

Before school

A quick lookup. Three good options that are on the shelf. No long training. Just enough to help the next child in line.

Between classes

A few minutes, a few kids. The librarian still has the conversation. The tool just means they aren’t guessing from sticky notes alone.

Friday rush

If a popular book is checked out, it isn’t offered as something to recommend out loud. We don’t send a child hunting for an empty shelf.

A fourth grader who loves graphic stories walks up. The desk shows a few next titles that are in. The librarian says, “Try this one,” and the child leaves with a book in hand.

How we’d bring it in

Carefully, with librarians at the table from day one.

WhenWhat we’d do
First 1–2 weeksSit with librarians and privacy/IT. Look at the real catalog and borrow files. Agree what stays private. Watch the desk for a morning.
Next 3–4 weeksConnect those files to a simple desk screen. Practice the before-school and Friday paths. Keep any auto-written talking points off until privacy says yes.
1–2 weeks after thatTrain with the people who will actually use it. Rehearse what happens if a helper feature is down — book lists still work.
About 6–8 weeksTry it in one school or grade band. Watch whether staff use it, whether it slows them down, and whether kids leave with books they want to read.

All told, plan on about 11 to 16 weeks for a first school pilot. School calendars and approvals can stretch that. This is a planning range, not a hard bid.

Who keeps it going

In the district

  • Library lead decides what “a good recommendation” means
  • IT handles access, file loads, and where it runs
  • Privacy lead decides what can leave the building
  • Principals protect a little librarian time to use it well

On our side

  • Fix problems quickly
  • Leave a clear how-to guide
  • Make sure the desk still works if optional extras are turned off
  • Report honestly — including when something isn’t working

After the pilot, the district decides: keep it, change it, or stop. If it doesn’t help at the desk, we shouldn’t keep it.

We already have a working desk demo for anyone who wants to see how it hangs together. That demo doesn’t prove reading will jump overnight. Checking a book out isn’t the same as finishing it — we’ll be honest about what we measure.

Hosting and any paid helper services need real volume numbers later. Grades and counselor systems are a separate conversation, with separate approvals.

What I’m asking

Please choose this as our pro-bono project this year.

If you do, the next step is a week with the librarians: walk their mornings, agree on privacy, and look at their real files before we promise anything tighter.

  • We will not put this in a child’s hands as an app.
  • We will not try to replace the library system they already use.
  • We will not claim miracle reading gains from a short demo.

We want more kids to hear “I think you’ll like this one” — and walk out with a book that’s actually on the shelf.
